# Reasoning and Specificity

Use this reference to plan or review the report's argument. Adapt it to the discipline and rubric; do not force every paragraph into one template.

## Complete the important reasoning

For each major claim, make the necessary links explicit:

1. **Claim:** What is the report asserting or deciding?
2. **Evidence:** What specific supplied result, source, calculation, or requirement supports it?
3. **Explanation:** Why does that evidence support the claim? State the relevant mechanism or comparison.
4. **Implication:** Why does it matter for the question, system, user, model, or decision?
5. **Decision or conclusion:** What follows, and under what conditions?
6. **Limit:** What does the evidence not establish?

Use only the links needed for that claim. The goal is sufficient reasoning, not maximum length.

Examples of subject-neutral adaptations:

- Experiment: result → comparison with baseline → explanation → performance implication → limitation.
- Software design: requirement → selected design → technical reason → trade-off → consequence.
- Data analysis: evidence → interpretation → analytical implication → decision → residual uncertainty.
- Security: threat or weakness → evidence → impact mechanism → control → remaining risk.

## Specificity tests

Ask of every important sentence or paragraph:

- Could this sentence be copied into an unrelated report unchanged? If yes, add the relevant subject, evidence, condition, or consequence.
- Could a marker reasonably ask “How?”, “Why?”, “Based on what?”, “Compared with what?”, or “So what?” If yes, answer it nearby or narrow the claim.
- Does the sentence name a result without interpreting it?
- Does it state an effect without explaining the mechanism?
- Does it name a recommendation without identifying who acts or what they do, when relevant?
- Does it claim improvement without naming the measure and comparator?

Words such as “effective,” “efficient,” “significant,” “robust,” “suitable,” and “improved” are not banned. They require a defined meaning and support. For quantitative work, state units, sample/population, timeframe, denominator, and baseline when material and available.

## Claim status and certainty

Keep these categories distinct:

- **Observed:** directly shown by supplied material or results.
- **Calculated:** derived transparently from supplied values.
- **Inferred:** a reasoned interpretation, with its basis stated.
- **Proposed:** a suggested future action or target, not an achieved result.
- **Unknown:** not established by current evidence.

Use wording that matches the status. Do not convert correlation into cause, a proposed target into a measured outcome, or a plausible explanation into a verified fact. If a key link is unknown, use the evidence gate and ask the user rather than inventing it.

## Review outcome

When reviewing, report the most consequential breaks first. For each one, identify:

- the relevant requirement or claim;
- what the draft currently says;
- the missing evidence or reasoning link;
- why it affects correctness, persuasiveness, or the rubric;
- the smallest useful repair.

Do not inflate minor preferences into correctness failures. Distinguish factual error, unsupported claim, incomplete reasoning, vague wording, and optional stylistic improvement.
